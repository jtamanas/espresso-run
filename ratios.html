Espresso ratio and recipe cheat sheet

Typical dose, yield and time for ristretto, normale and lungo. Everything here is a starting point to adjust to your coffee, your machine and your taste.

The three numbers

  • Dose is the weight of dry ground coffee you put in the basket, in grams.
  • Yield is the weight of liquid espresso in the cup, in grams. Weigh it: crema makes volume unreliable.
  • Ratio is dose to yield. 18 g in and 36 g out is 1:2.
  • Time is how long the shot runs, measured here from when you start the pump (the shot timer does this for you).

Use a dose your basket is designed for. Many home double baskets are made for roughly 14 to 20 g, but check the markings on yours or its manual. Overfilling or underfilling a basket causes problems that no recipe can fix.

Ristretto, normale and lungo

Starting-point ranges. Names and exact numbers vary between cafes, countries and people.
StyleRatio (dose:yield)Time from pump startGrindTypically tastes
Ristretto ("restricted") About 1:1 to 1:1.5 Roughly 15 to 25 s: nearer 15 to 20 s if you keep the normale grind and stop early, nearer 25 s if you grind finer Fine, often a touch finer than for normale Concentrated, syrupy, sweet-leaning. Can be harsh or unbalanced if pushed too far.
Normale (standard espresso) About 1:2 (roughly 1:1.7 to 1:2.4, the same band the dial-in helper treats as normal) Roughly 25 to 30 s Fine The balanced middle: body, sweetness and some acidity. The usual place to start.
Lungo ("long") About 1:2.5 to 1:4 Roughly 35 to 50 s Slightly coarser, so a long shot does not choke or turn bitter Larger, lighter and more diluted in body, with more bitterness if over-extracted.

A traditional single shot uses roughly 7 g of coffee and a traditional double roughly 14 g. Modern specialty-style recipes often use 16 to 20 g in a double basket, as in the table below.

A first recipe to try

  1. Weigh 18 g of fresh beans (or whatever dose suits your basket) and grind them just before brewing.
  2. Distribute the grounds evenly and tamp level.
  3. Put the cup on a scale and tare it. Start the pump and start the timer.
  4. Stop the shot when the cup shows about 36 g (a 1:2 ratio). Note the time.
  5. Taste it. Sour or thin and fast: grind finer. Bitter or harsh and slow: grind coarser. Change one thing at a time, and log what you did in the dial-in helper.

Rules of thumb for adjusting

  • Lighter roasts often taste better with a longer ratio (for example 1:2.5 or more) and sometimes a finer grind or hotter water. Darker roasts often taste better at shorter ratios (about 1:1.5 to 1:2) and, if your machine allows, cooler water.
  • Milk drinks are often made with a shorter, more concentrated shot (around 1:1.5 to 1:2) so the coffee still comes through the milk.
  • Longer yield at the same grind and dose usually gives more extraction, a little less body and more clarity. Shorter yield does the opposite.
  • Grind size is the main control for time. Finer slows the shot, coarser speeds it up. Yield is the main control for strength and balance.
  • Beans change as they age and every machine runs a bit differently, so expect to re-adjust with each new bag.
